# Didactic Roadmap (grammar/functional syllabus — language-agnostic)

`roadmap.md` answers "what will the learner read about, at each level?" (theme, chapter,
topic). This file answers a different question that `roadmap.md` deliberately doesn't
cover: **"what grammar/function does the learner need to actually *have* before a topic's
texts can be written naturally?"**

This gap is what broke chapter_1/topic_1 of `pt.json`: the topic taught greeting
vocabulary correctly, but nothing anywhere in the curriculum ever taught basic
connectives (`e`, `a`, `que`, `com`...), so no topic past it could ever be written as a
real, grammatical sentence without silently reaching outside its own word list. A thematic
roadmap alone can't catch that kind of gap — it never asks "can a sentence actually be
built from this yet?"

Like `roadmap.md`, this file is shared/language-agnostic: the functional/grammatical
progression below is standard CEFR practice and doesn't vary by target language (only the
concrete word list per topic, derived from this later, is language-specific).

**Status: level-level only.** Per-chapter/topic assignment (which specific topic is
responsible for teaching which item below) is the next pass, once this level-level shape
is agreed. Nothing in `pt.json` is changed by this document alone — it's the reference
`pt.json`'s per-topic word lists get audited and redefined against, topic by topic,
afterward.

## How to read each level's section

For each level, two kinds of content are listed separately, because they get taught
differently:

- **Structural items** — closed-class words and patterns that exist independent of any
  topic's theme (pronouns, articles, connectives, question formation, tense/aspect,
  negation). These need a *deliberate* home somewhere, or they silently never get taught,
  exactly like the connectives gap.
- **Thematic-adjacent items** — content that CEFR expects at this level and that also
  happens to map cleanly onto specific existing `roadmap.md` topics (numbers, days,
  colors, measurements). These aren't structural, but they're still being called out here
  because they're easy to under-teach if left purely to "whichever topic happens to need
  one example."

## A0 (pre-A1 — this project's own on-ramp level)

Official CEFR starts at A1; this project's A0 is the same true-beginner on-ramp
`roadmap.md` already defines it as (Section 5: "simple present tense, no subordination,
~500–2,000 word vocabulary ceiling"). Everything here exists to make that ceiling actually
reachable — a learner leaving A0 should be able to build a short, grammatically complete,
present-tense sentence about themselves or their surroundings, unaided.

**Structural items:**

- Subject pronouns (I, you, he/she/it, we, they — both informal/formal 2nd person where
  the target language distinguishes them).
- Present tense of "to be" and "to have," all persons actually needed for A0 sentences
  (not just 1st person — this is exactly the `estou`/`está` gap found in topic_1).
- Definite and indefinite articles, with gender/number agreement where the language has
  it (the `bom`/`boa` gap).
- The core coordinating connectives: "and," "or" (not yet "but"/"because" — those are A1
  per `roadmap.md`'s own A1-EL description).
- Simple negation ("not," "no").
- Yes/no question formation, and short yes/no answers.
- The basic wh-question words (what, where, who, how, when) in their simplest form.
- Possessive adjectives (my, your, his/her), at least the forms needed for the nouns
  already in play by A0.
- Basic demonstratives (this/that).
- Plural formation (regular pattern).
- Basic prepositions of place (in, on, at, under, near/far — already partly covered by
  `roadmap.md`'s A0-TR direction vocabulary, but the prepositions themselves need to be
  explicit structural items, not incidental).
- Simple present-tense conjugation for regular verbs, plus the handful of high-frequency
  irregular verbs A0's own topics already depend on (go, do, want, can).
- "There is/there are."

**Thematic-adjacent items (CEFR-expected at A0, each maps onto an existing topic):**

- Cardinal numbers 0–100 → `roadmap.md` A0-EL-6.
- Basic telling of time, days of the week → A0-EL-6.
- Simple prices/quantities ("how much") → A0-EL-6.
- Colors → currently only appears inside A0-CT-3 (Clothes); worth confirming that's
  colors' only real estate at A0, since color is also a natural fit for A0-NS
  (nature/animals) — a chapter/topic-level question for the next pass.
- Basic shapes — not currently anchored to any A0-CT/A0-NS topic; flagging as a possible
  gap, to check at the chapter/topic pass.

## A1

CEFR: "can understand sentences and frequently used expressions... can communicate in
simple and routine tasks... can describe in simple terms aspects of their background."
`roadmap.md`'s own A1 chapter descriptions already name some of this (A1-EL: "simple
compound sentences and light connectors (and, then, because)") — this section makes that
explicit and complete rather than incidental to one chapter's description.

**Structural items:**

- The remaining core connectives: "but," "because," plus sequencing connectors ("first,"
  "then," "after that," "finally" — A1-SS's whole reason for being, per `roadmap.md`).
- Simple past tense (regular forms, plus the irregular verbs already load-bearing by A1's
  topics — go, do, have, be, say).
- Near-future construction ("going to" / equivalent).
- Core modal verbs: can, must/have to, want to.
- Simple comparatives ("bigger than," "as...as").
- Frequency adverbs (always, sometimes, never, usually).
- Reflexive verb conjugation (already needed by A0-EL-4's routine verbs — confirm it's
  taught as a *pattern*, not memorized per-verb).
- Direct object pronouns, at least singular (needed the moment any topic says "I see
  it"/"I buy it" instead of repeating the noun).
- Simple affirmative/negative short-answer patterns beyond yes/no (e.g. "I do" / "I
  don't").
- Basic imperative (commands) — load-bearing for A1-TR's direction-giving topics.
- Quantifiers: a lot, a little, some, none.

**Thematic-adjacent items:**

- Units of measurement (weight, distance, size) — natural fit for A1-EL-4 (Shopping for
  Groceries) and A1-TR topics; currently not anchored anywhere.
- Months, dates — natural fit for A1-TR-4 (Booking a Simple Trip) or A1-CT topics;
  currently not anchored anywhere.
- Ordinal numbers (first, second, third) — needed by A1-TR-2's "multi-step directions"
  and useful for dates; not currently anchored.

## A2

CEFR: "can communicate in simple and routine tasks requiring a direct exchange of
information... can describe in simple terms aspects of their background, immediate
environment."

**Structural items:**

- Full past tense system for the target language (e.g. preterite vs. imperfect
  distinction, if the language marks it) — A0/A1 only need "a" simple past; A2 is where
  the *contrast* between two past aspects becomes relevant.
- Simple future tense (distinct from A1's "going to" construction).
- Comparative and superlative, fully (not just the A1 basic comparative).
- Temporal/causal subordinators: "when," "while," "although," "since" (time sense).
- Basic first-conditional pattern ("if + present, ... future").
- Indirect/embedded questions ("I don't know where he is," vs. A0/A1's direct "Where is
  he?").
- Additional modals: should, might, could (advice/possibility, beyond A1's
  can/must/want).
- Possessive pronouns, distinct from A0's possessive adjectives (mine/yours vs. my/your).
- Basic relative clauses (who, which, that) — the first real step toward subordination
  beyond simple connectors.
- Adverbs of manner, and their regular formation pattern.
- A wider preposition set for time (during, before, after, since, for).

**Thematic-adjacent items:**

- Nothing new flagged at A2 — this level is mostly structural deepening of A0/A1's
  existing thematic coverage rather than introducing new thematic categories.

## B1

CEFR: "can deal with most situations likely to arise... can produce simple connected text
on topics which are familiar... can describe experiences and events, dreams, hopes and
ambitions and briefly give reasons and explanations for opinions and plans."

**Structural items:**

- Present perfect, and its contrast with simple past (a genuinely new aspectual
  distinction, not just "more past tense").
- Full conditional sentences: first and second conditional properly contrasted
  (real/hypothetical present).
- Basic reported speech (he said that..., she asked if...).
- Passive voice, introduced (simple present/past passive).
- Modal verbs for obligation/possibility/deduction, fuller (must be, might have,
  should've).
- Contrast/cause/consequence connectors: however, therefore, despite, so that.
- Gerund vs. infinitive distinction after verbs (want to do vs. enjoy doing).
- First genuinely idiomatic set phrases — `roadmap.md`'s own Section 5 sketch already
  calls this out as the level where idiom starts; this is where it should be deliberately
  introduced, not incidental.

**Thematic-adjacent items:**

- None newly flagged — B1 is the level where roadmap.md's own coarse sketch (Section 5)
  takes over content direction; thematic breadth mostly comes from chapter variety, not
  new closed-class categories.

## B2

CEFR: "can understand the main ideas of complex text... can interact with a degree of
fluency and spontaneity... can produce clear, detailed text on a wide range of subjects."

**Structural items:**

- Relative clauses, fuller (non-restrictive clauses, relative pronouns beyond
  who/which/that).
- All conditional types, including third conditional and mixed conditionals.
- Subjunctive mood, if the target language has one and hasn't needed it before now
  (e.g. Spanish/Portuguese subjunctive triggers — wishes, doubt, emotion, certain
  conjunctions).
- Reported speech, fuller (tense backshifting across more tenses, reported
  questions/commands).
- Nominalization (turning verbs/adjectives into nouns for more formal registers).
- Cohesive devices for structured/formal writing (furthermore, in contrast, as a result).
- First real exposure to regional/dialectal variety, if the target language has
  meaningful regional variation — `roadmap.md`'s own coarse sketch already flags this at
  B2.

## C1

CEFR: "can understand a wide range of demanding, longer texts... can express ideas
fluently and spontaneously without much searching for expressions... can use language
flexibly and effectively for social, academic and professional purposes."

**Structural items:**

- Full command of the subjunctive (all trigger categories, not just the B2 basics).
- Complex discourse markers and register-shifting connectors.
- Dense subordination — multiple embedded/nested clauses in a single sentence.
- Nuanced register control: formal vs. informal grammar choices made deliberately, not
  just vocabulary-level politeness.
- Idiomatic and figurative language, fuller — beyond B1's "first set phrases," genuine
  figurative/metaphorical usage.
- Comfortable navigation of dialectal variety introduced at B2.

## C2

CEFR: "can understand with ease virtually everything heard or read... can express
themselves spontaneously, very fluently and precisely, differentiating finer shades of
meaning even in the most complex situations."

No new grammatical categories are introduced at C2 — by this point the structural
inventory from A0–C1 is complete. What C2 adds is **precision and range within** that
inventory: finer register/nuance control, cross-genre fluency (legal, academic, literary),
full comfort with implicit meaning, and stylistic choices that go beyond correctness into
deliberate effect. This matches `roadmap.md`'s own coarse-sketch description of C2 almost
verbatim, so no separate structural list is warranted here — C2's "syllabus" is really
"everything below, at native-like control."

# A0 — Per-Topic Breakdown

This assigns every A0 structural/thematic item above to one of `roadmap.md`'s 34 A0
topics. It's the reference the upcoming `pt.json` topic-by-topic pass (chapter_1/topic_1
already done) will be checked against — nothing in `pt.json` is changed by this section
alone.

Two kinds of note appear inline, both worth reading before editing `pt.json` against this:

- **Assignment** — which topic is now responsible for teaching a given structural/
  thematic item, and why that topic (not another) is the natural home for it.
- **Flag** — something this analysis surfaced in `pt.json`'s *current* state while
  checking each topic against its assignment: a genuine data bug, a placement that
  contradicts the assignment below, or an open question with no clearly-better answer yet.
  Flags are observations, not decisions — none of them are applied to `pt.json` here.

## Cross-cutting decisions made in this pass

Two items needed a single decision applied consistently across topics, rather than being
re-litigated per topic:

1. **Connectives distribution.** `e` ("and") → A0-EL-1 (earliest possible topic, per the
   chapter_1/topic_1 discussion). `o`/`a` (definite articles) → A0-EL-2, where every noun
   introduced has an unambiguous, easily-modeled gender (father/mother, brother/sister).
   `um`/`uma` (indefinite articles) → A0-EL-3, where "a room," "a table" are exactly the
   kind of first-mention noun phrases that topic's content already produces. `que`
   ("that"/"what") → A0-SS-3 (Meeting Someone New), the first topic built around genuine
   back-and-forth questions ("¿What do you do?", "¿What's your name?"). This spreads the
   four hardest-to-place items across the four earliest topics instead of loading them
   all onto topic_1 — open to revisiting once the word-budget tension below is resolved.
2. **Imperative mood tension.** A0-TR-1 (Asking for Directions) and A0-TG-2 (Using a
   Computer) are described in `roadmap.md` as producing direction-giving/instructional
   text — the natural register for that is imperative ("turn left," "click here"), but
   this document's A0 structural list deliberately keeps imperative at A1, to hold A0 to
   simple-present-only per `roadmap.md`'s own Section 5 definition. Recommendation: keep
   both topics descriptive/present-tense at A0 ("the street is to the left," "you click
   here") rather than pulling imperative down a level for just two topics — but this is a
   real trade-off, not an obvious call, and worth confirming before the `pt.json` pass
   touches either topic.
3. **Gender-pair local completeness (revised — supersedes the original A0-EL-3 `um`/`uma`
   framing below).** A gendered word's counterpart form(s) must be present *within the same
   topic's own word list*, not merely reusable from another topic elsewhere in `pt.json`.
   The original pass treated `um` as covered for A0-EL-3 because it already existed in
   A0-EL-6 — but a learner studying "At Home" in isolation needs to be able to say "a room"
   /"a table" without having already studied numbers. Applied to A0-EL-3: `um` is now added
   directly to that topic's own list alongside `uma`, not left as a cross-topic reuse.

actually generated for this topic.

# A0 — Per-Topic Word-Count Targets

CEFR doesn't define A0 directly (it's this project's own pre-A1 on-ramp), but the
commonly-cited ~500–1,000 word "beginner survival vocabulary" figure, split across a
level that's meant to be a fraction of that, is what `roadmap.md`'s own Section 4 already
anchors to: "~15–20 new words/topic, ~34 topics... roughly 510–680 words." The targets
below are that same rule applied **per topic instead of flat**, using each topic's actual
content richness (how many genuinely distinct, non-overlapping concepts it naturally
needs) rather than a uniform 15–20 everywhere — a numbers/family/food topic naturally
needs more discrete items than a one-line-headline topic does.

**Target total: 568 words** (within the requested 500–700 range, and inside
`roadmap.md`'s own 510–680 estimate).

## Reality check against current `pt.json`
